Robust.

Timeless by design, not tied to this year's framework.

  • No vendor lock-in. Primitives and components built on the platform itself, so they outlive whichever framework is fashionable at the moment. Nothing here is generated to match a trend.
  • Built to grow with the platform. Made to adopt what browsers ship as they ship it, with fallbacks for every browser we support, and accessible best practices built in from the start rather than bolted on afterwards.
  • And as performant as that allows. Growing with the platform is what keeps them small and quick: there is no wrapper runtime between your application and the browser.
  • Accessibility as a contract. Every component carries screen-reader tests, not a promise in a README.
  • Written by UI and accessibility specialists with more than ten years in the field. People who follow the specifications closely, and who build for the best experience a UI library can offer. Thousands of projects already build on them, so regressions surface early and loudly.

Flexible.

Headless before the term was even invented.

Seven years of hardening, giving you the hardened, accessible abstractions you need.

  • More than theming. Not a palette swap: the components carry a full visual identity, and you can replace every part of it.
  • It fits what you already have. Whatever stack pre-existed — other CSS libraries, your own design system, a legacy application — it slots in beside it.

User Interfaces.

Systems, primitives and components.

Three levels, one direction: start from a primitive when you need control, from a component when you need to ship.

  • Systems. The behaviour that is hard to get right — forms, overlays, localisation, validation.
  • Primitives. The small pieces those systems are built from, usable on their own.
  • Components. The finished controls, accessible and styled, ready to drop in.

Everywhere.

Framework agnostic, by construction.

Web components and framework-agnostic controllers: the behaviour does not belong to a framework, so it does not leave with one.

  • Web components. The components are custom elements, so any framework that renders HTML can use them.
  • Framework-agnostic controllers. The logic underneath is reusable without the element, for teams writing their own rendering layer.
